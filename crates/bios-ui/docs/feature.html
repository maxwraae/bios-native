A feature is an annotated span of DNA: a stretch of bases, and something said about what it is. A promoter is a feature. A CDS is a feature. So is a primer, a terminator, an origin, a homology arm, and the whole plasmid.

That is not a simplification for the documentation. It is the actual object model, and it is worth stating in full because everything else follows from it:

A feature is an annotated span of DNA. A feature can contain features. A feature can have variants.

Why there is only one kind of thing

Most sequence tools make you sort your work into categories: annotations here, a separate parts library there, and some act of conversion between them. Bios has no second object. Nothing gets promoted into being reusable and nothing gets converted into being swappable.

The practical consequence is that scale is not type. A promoter, a whole expression cassette and the finished plasmid are the same kind of thing at different sizes, so anything you can do to one you can do to the others. You can annotate inside a cassette that is itself annotated, because a feature contains features.

Seeing what is on your construct

Features are drawn on the map, and there are three ways to inspect them.

  • Click one on the map. A card tells you what it is and what it is for, and selects that span in the sequence view.
  • Double click it. The edit form opens: name, category, direction, start and end, and the free text describing what the feature serves and why it is there.
  • Open the Contents panel on the left for the full list, grouped and collapsible. Clicking a row selects it on the map.

What is drawn is under your control. The Show on map popover carries the layer toggles and the label density, so a construct with sixty annotations can be read at whatever level of detail you actually want.

Annotating a new one

Select a stretch of sequence and give it a name. A floating readout follows any selection with its length, GC content, melting temperature and how many times those bases occur in the construct, which is usually enough to tell you whether the span you have grabbed is the one you meant.

You can also ask the assistant to annotate for you. Because that adds something to the construct it arrives as a proposal rather than as a change, so you see the span it picked before it exists.

Features that are primers

A primer is a feature like any other, with one convenience on top: turn a selection into a named primer with Cmd P, and Bios reads the direction from the way you dragged. Hovering a primer gives you its melting temperature, GC, length and whether it binds anywhere else it should not.

This is what makes the Order button work. It harvests every primer annotated on the construct into a CSV, deduplicated and with the numbers already filled in, so your order list is derived from the design rather than transcribed from it.
